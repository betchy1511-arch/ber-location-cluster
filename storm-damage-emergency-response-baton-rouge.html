Emergency response is a different job from roofing, and treating it as the first step of a roof repair is how people get hurt. What has actually happened is that a building has been hit, and the first question is whether it is safe to be in and safe to work on. That is answered from the ground and from inside before anybody puts a ladder against it.

The second thing is that the situation is evidence, and evidence has a short life. Water stops running, debris gets cleared, a tarp goes over the hole, and within a day the only record of what the storm did is whatever somebody photographed. That is why the photographs come before the tarp rather than after it, even though every instinct says cover it first. It costs minutes and it is the part nobody can go back and do again.

What not to touch until somebody has looked

Four things people reasonably do straight after a storm that quietly make the situation worse. None of them is obvious and none is careless, which is exactly why they are worth saying plainly rather than assuming everyone already knows. Two are safety, and two are about not destroying the record of what happened.

Do not go into standing water indoors

Not until the power is off

Water on a floor under a damaged ceiling can be in contact with wiring or a fallen fitting. If the power cannot be isolated safely from outside the affected area, that is a reason to stay out and call, not a reason to be careful.

Do not push a bulging ceiling

It is holding water

A ceiling sagging under trapped water can release the whole load at once. It is dealt with deliberately, from the right position, with the room cleared, rather than poked to see how bad it is.

Do not clear up before photographing

The clear up destroys the record

Sweeping debris, moving furniture and covering openings all happen eventually. Doing them before anything is photographed removes the only evidence of what the storm did, and it cannot be reconstructed.

Do not climb up to look

Especially not on a wet roof

A roof that has lost fixings can look intact and give way under weight, and a wet roof after a storm is the worst combination of conditions. Whatever is up there will still be there when somebody with the right equipment arrives.

Be careful about who arrives at the door

A storm brings people to the door, and some of them were not in Baton Rouge the week before. Check the contractor is licensed by the Louisiana State Licensing Board for Contractors, get any price in writing with the date on it, and do not sign anything that assigns your claim or your benefits to the contractor.

Emergency pressure is exactly the condition under which people sign things they would not otherwise sign. A contractor who is willing to make the roof safe and talk about the repair afterwards is behaving normally; one who needs a signature before starting is telling you something.

Price gouging is regulated during a declared emergency

La. R.S. 29:732 restricts prices during a declared state of emergency. The part worth knowing is that the restriction runs for a limited period and is renewed only by specific reference, so whether it is in force on any given day depends on the declaration in effect at the time.

That is not a reason to accept a price you are unhappy with, and it is not legal advice. It is a reason to get the figure in writing with a date on it, which is the thing that lets anyone assess it later.

What a named storm does to how fast anyone can reach you

Being honest about response times after a widespread event is more useful than a promise nobody can keep. Three things about a regional storm change the picture, they are the same for every contractor working in this market, and knowing them makes it easier to judge whoever turns up at your door offering a firm time.

The whole region calls in the same hour

A single damaged house is a scheduling question. A named storm crossing the parish means every roofer in the area has the same queue on the same morning, and no honest answer involves a specific hour.

What we will do is tell you where you are in that queue and what to do in the meantime, which is more useful than a time that then moves. An active leak into a living space is triaged differently from damage that is contained.

The damage keeps growing until it is covered

The storm is the first event. The rain that follows it is the second, and on a roof with an opening in it that second event does most of the interior damage. That is the reason covering matters even when the repair is weeks away.

It is also why a temporary cover is a real piece of work rather than a sheet thrown over a hole. Our emergency roofing page sets out what that involves and what it costs.

Older housing stock takes wind differently

Baton Rouge has a lot of older housing, and an older covering that has lost its seal strip in a previous storm can lift in a wind that a newer roof shrugs off. The damage often does not look dramatic from the ground.

That is a reason to have someone look after a storm even when nothing is obviously wrong, rather than a reason to assume the worst. A broken seal strip lies back down looking untouched and leaks the following season.

What should I do first after a storm damages my roof

Establish whether the building is safe to be in, before anything else. Stay out of standing water indoors until the power can be isolated safely, do not push a bulging ceiling because it is holding water, and do not climb up to look. Then photograph everything before anyone clears up or covers anything.

Can I just put a tarp on it myself

We would rather you did not, and the reason is not liability. A roof that has lost fixings can look intact and give way under weight, and it is wet, which is the worst combination of conditions there is. Whatever is up there will still be there when someone with the right equipment arrives.

Why photograph before covering the damage

Because a tarp hides exactly the thing somebody will later want to see, and it cannot be recreated. Photographing takes minutes and it is the only record of what the storm actually did. Everything else on the list can be done in a different order; this one cannot.

Does emergency work need a permit in East Baton Rouge

Make safe work does not. A full reroof does, and it is inspected, under La. R.S. 37:3737(I). Which body issues it depends on the address, because St. George, Baker, Zachary and Central permit their own.
