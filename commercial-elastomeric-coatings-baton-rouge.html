Baton Rouge elastomeric coatings at a glance

An elastomeric coating is a liquid applied film that stretches and recovers as the roof moves. The acrylic type is the subject of an ASTM standard. Service life depends on several things, and the one easiest to leave out of a quote is whether the details were reinforced with fabric.

Where coatings tend to fail

The details

Rather than the open field. Seams, curbs, drains, parapet junctions and penetrations are where the roof moves most and where the film sits thinnest over an edge, so those are the places a specification has to name.

Who issues the permit

East Baton Rouge Parish

For Baton Rouge and the unincorporated parish. The City of St. George took applications filed on or after 16 October 2024, and Baker, Zachary and Central run their own. A coating over a sound covering is maintenance rather than the re roofing the 2025 rule covers.

The standard

ASTM D6083/D6083M

The standard specification for liquid applied acrylic roof coating. It is published in revised editions, so a datasheet that names the standard without naming the edition has told you less than it appears to.

What it does not cover

Anything but acrylic

The standard is written for water dispersed acrylic. IIBEC records that it explicitly excludes styrenated, urethane, silicone, butyl and vinyl acetate polymers, so elastomeric printed on a silicone pail is not a claim against it.

The reinforcement

Polyester fabric

Embedded into the coating at every detail rather than laid over the top. It is a small share of the material cost and a large share of how long the system holds together.

The scheduling constraint

The cure window

A water dispersed coating needs a dry spell to cure to the properties the standard describes, and Baton Rouge weather narrows that window, so the roof is staged in sections around the forecast.

Elastomeric is a description of behaviour rather than a brand: the cured film stretches and recovers instead of cracking when the roof underneath it expands and contracts. That matters because a rigid film on a moving roof splits at the points of greatest movement, and the points of greatest movement are the seams, the curbs and the parapet junctions, which are exactly the places a coating is there to protect.

For the acrylic type there is an ASTM standard behind the word, currently published as D6083/D6083M, which sets minimum properties for the cured film including elongation, tensile strength, adhesion, permeance and low temperature flexibility after weathering. Two things are worth knowing about it. It has been revised more than once, so the edition claimed is part of the claim. And it covers water dispersed acrylic only, so a silicone or urethane product described as elastomeric is not making the same statement. Big Easy Roofing names the product and the edition of the standard it is stated to meet, and where the right answer for a roof is a different chemistry we say so rather than stretching the word to fit.

Reading the standard, and who permits the work

The standard tells you how a cured film behaves in a laboratory. It does not tell you the roof is a candidate, that the substrate will hold the coating, or that acrylic suits your drainage. Those are survey questions, and the permit position is separate again.

The standard

ASTM D6083/D6083M

The standard specification for liquid applied acrylic roof coating. Ask which edition a datasheet claims, because it has been revised more than once and the current edition is the 2024 one. The numeric minimums live in the standard itself, which is a paid document.

The limit of it

A film test

Compliance describes the cured film under laboratory conditions. It says nothing about whether your roof is a candidate, how the coating will adhere to your particular substrate or whether the drainage suits an acrylic at all. It is a floor for the product, not a verdict on the installation.

Elastomeric is not one thing

The word describes how a film behaves, not what it is made of, and only one chemistry has this particular standard behind it. IIBEC records that D6083 is written for water dispersed acrylic and explicitly excludes styrenated, urethane, silicone, butyl and vinyl acetate polymers.

So a silicone coating can be perfectly elastomeric in behaviour and perfectly right for a roof that holds water, while not being a D6083 product at all. Which chemistry suits a given roof is a drainage and exposure question, set out on the roof coating page. What matters here is that the two claims do not get treated as the same claim.

What to ask a bidder for

Three lines, and they cost a bidder nothing to supply. The product name. The edition of the standard the datasheet claims. And where reinforcing fabric is being installed, listed by location rather than described as included.

The third is the one that separates two quotes that otherwise read alike. A bid with no reinforcement at the seams, curbs and drains is usually cheaper because it is a smaller job, and the details are where that difference would tell rather than on the day it is finished.

What a Baton Rouge roof does to an elastomeric film

Two local conditions decide how an elastomeric system performs here, and they pull against each other. The roof moves every day, which is the argument for a film that stretches. And the weather is wet, which narrows the window in which a water dispersed film can cure to the properties it is specified for.

Daily movement

A low slope commercial roof in this climate warms hard under summer sun and gives that heat back overnight, and the assembly expands and contracts with it every day of its life. A film that cannot follow that movement does not fail evenly. It opens at the seams and the angle changes first, which is the argument for both the elastomeric behaviour and the fabric at the details.

The cure window

A water dispersed coating cures by losing water to the air, so it needs a dry spell after application to reach the properties the standard describes. The National Weather Service 1991 to 2020 normal for Baton Rouge Ryan Field is 61.94 inches of rain a year, with June the wettest month at 6.45 inches, and it tends to arrive in short heavy bursts rather than steadily.

That does not stop the work, it schedules it. The roof is staged in sections sized to what can be applied and left to cure inside the forecast, rather than opened up wholesale. A film rained on before it has cured has not simply been delayed, it may not reach the specification it was written to.

Standing water

Acrylic based films are generally the less tolerant of water that sits, and ponding resistance varies between products, so on a roof that holds water we read the documents for that particular system rather than relying on the standard, and check what its warranty says about ponding. Where the answer is a different chemistry we say so. Drainage that can be corrected is better corrected than coated around.

What is ASTM D6083

It is the ASTM standard specification for liquid applied acrylic roof coating, currently published as D6083/D6083M. It sets minimum properties for the cured film, including elongation, tensile strength, adhesion, permeance and low temperature flexibility after weathering. The numeric requirements are inside the standard itself, which is a paid document.

Which edition should I ask for

Ask which one the datasheet claims, because the standard has been revised more than once and the current edition is the 2024 one. A product sheet that says it meets the standard without naming an edition has said less than it appears to, and it is a fair and easy question to put to any bidder.

Does the standard apply to silicone coatings

No. It is written for water dispersed acrylic, and IIBEC records that it explicitly excludes styrenated, urethane, silicone, butyl and vinyl acetate polymers. A silicone coating can still be the right choice for your roof, particularly where water stands on it, but a claim against this standard is not what makes it right.

Does meeting the standard mean the coating will work on my roof

It does not. The standard describes how the cured film behaves in a laboratory. It says nothing about whether your roof is a candidate, whether the insulation under the membrane is dry, or how the coating will adhere to your particular substrate. Those are survey questions and they are settled before a product is chosen.

Where do coatings usually fail

At the details rather than in the open field. Seams, curbs, drains, parapet junctions and penetrations are where the roof moves most and where the film ends up thinnest over an edge. A coating that has opened up is far more often a detailing problem than a product problem.

What is the polyester fabric for

It is embedded into the coating at the details to carry the movement that would otherwise be carried by the film alone. It adds thickness and tensile strength exactly where both are most needed. It is a small share of the material cost on a roof and a large share of how long the system holds together.

Two elastomeric quotes look the same. What separates them

Usually the reinforcement, and it is an easy line to leave out. Ask each bidder to list where fabric is being installed by location rather than to say it is included. A quote with no fabric at the seams, curbs and drains is usually cheaper because it is a smaller job, and the details are where that difference would tell.

Does the weather here affect the application

My roof holds water after rain. Is elastomeric right for it

Probably not. Acrylic based films soften under water that sits, so on a roof that ponds we would specify a chemistry that tolerates standing water instead, and look at whether the drainage itself can be corrected. Which chemistry suits which roof is covered on the roof coating page.

Does an elastomeric coating need a permit in East Baton Rouge

Normally not, because the permit follows the work rather than the product. La. R.S. 37:3737(I) bites on re roofing, and leaving the existing covering in place under a coating is not that.
