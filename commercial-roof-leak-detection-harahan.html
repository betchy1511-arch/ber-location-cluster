Harahan commercial roof leak detection at a glance

A commercial leak is diagnosed by finding the breach in the membrane, not by following the drip inside the building. Two instrumented methods do that: electronic leak detection under ASTM D7877 and D8231, and the controlled flood test. Each has cases where the other is the right method, and the survey picks between them.

The two methods

ELD and controlled flood

Electronic leak detection (ELD) energises the membrane with a small voltage and locates the breach where current passes through. The controlled flood test dams the roof and floods it, then watches where water comes through. ELD is quicker on a large open low-slope roof; flood is definitive on a small area where ELD cannot be used. Call Big Easy Roofing on (504) 800-8196.

The standards behind ELD

ASTM D7877 and D8231

ASTM D7877 is the standard guide for electronic methods for detecting and locating leaks in waterproof membranes. ASTM D8231 is the standard practice for a low-voltage electronic scanning system. Both are current ASTM standards. The edition you want on any scope of work is worth confirming, because the current edition is what the report should cite.

For the resulting repair or reroof

Leak detection itself is not permitted work. Any resulting repair or re-roof is permitted under Louisiana R.S. 37:3737(I)(1). What decides scope after we find it

Moisture survey

Finding the breach in the membrane is one question; how much of the insulation below is now wet is a separate question. The moisture survey behind that (infrared, capacitance, nuclear) lives on the commercial inspections page. A repair over saturated insulation is a repair with a date on it, so the two run together.

The interior drip is a starting point

Not the location

On a low-slope roof water enters at a breach, tracks laterally along the top of the deck or through saturated insulation, and appears inside the building at a point that can be a long way from the breach. So the interior drip location tells us the leak is real; it does not tell us where it is.

On a house the roof leaks by gravity. Water hits a sloped covering, finds a gap somewhere in that covering, and runs down the underside of the deck until it drips out somewhere close to the gap. The residential leak detection page (roof leak detection) is written around the small displacement between where water enters and where it drips.

On a low-slope commercial roof the physics is different. Water sits on the membrane after every rainfall, and where it finds a breach it goes through the membrane and then tracks laterally along the top of the deck or through the insulation beneath. It can appear inside the building at a point tens of feet from the actual breach, or at several points from one breach, or (on a saturated insulation area) not appear inside for a long time until the saturation reaches something that gives. So the diagnostic method has to find the breach in the membrane, not follow the drip inside, and that is what electronic leak detection and the controlled flood test are for.

Electronic leak detection against the controlled flood test

The two methods answer the same question by different physics, and each has cases where the other is wrong. What decides between them is the membrane type, the size and shape of the area to be tested, and whether the roof can carry the weight of the flood.

What leak detection is, and what it is not

A leak detection visit is a diagnostic to find the source of water entry through a commercial roof membrane. It is not a permit inspection, it is not a moisture survey (that is the commercial inspections page), and it is not an insurance interpretation.

Not a code inspection

That is on the reroof

Not a moisture survey

That is on the inspections page

Leak detection finds where water enters the membrane. Moisture survey measures how much of the insulation below the membrane is now wet. Those are two different questions with two different methods, and the commercial inspections page owns the moisture survey.

What the report contains

Breach location and repair scope

The location of the breach relative to a roof plan, photographs, the method used, the ASTM standard the method sits under, and a recommended repair scope. Where the finding pairs with a wet insulation zone, that is noted and the moisture-survey follow-up is scoped alongside.

Why the interior drip is misleading

A homeowner would trace a leak back from the drip; on a low-slope commercial roof that traces to the wrong place. Water enters at a breach in the membrane, then goes through the insulation or along the top of the deck, and appears at whatever point it finds an opening. That point can be tens of feet from the breach or, on a saturated insulation area, delayed by days.

So the interior drip is confirmation that a leak is real. It is not where the leak is. The instrumented methods find the breach directly, which is why they matter on a commercial roof and not on a house.

What ELD needs to work

ELD needs three things: an electrically insulating membrane (most single-ply and many built-up systems qualify), the membrane in electrical contact with wet insulation or the deck (which is why a completely dry roof can defeat the low-voltage method), and access to lay the conductive wire around the test area.

Where any of those three is missing, ELD is the wrong method and the controlled flood test is the fallback. The manufacturer position on ELD on a specific membrane is worth confirming before scheduling, because a small number of membrane systems have their own statement on the method.

When each method fails, and what we do about it

Both methods have failure modes that are worth naming plainly, because either one used in the wrong case produces a false negative that reads as "no leak found" when the leak is real. The point of naming the failure modes is to pick the right method first.

When ELD returns a false negative

ELD returns "no breach" when there is one if the membrane is not sufficiently in contact with wet insulation or the deck (dry-scanning a completely dry roof), if the membrane is conductive across its whole surface (foil-faced systems), or if the test area is broken up into small compartments that the scanner cannot resolve. The fix in each case is the controlled flood test on a defined area.

When the flood test is not appropriate

The controlled flood test cannot run where the structure cannot carry the flood load, where the interior below the test area is sensitive to water, or where the test area is large enough that damming it is impractical. On any of those, ELD is the answer, or a targeted flood on a much smaller zone once ELD has localised the breach.

When neither method finds anything

That happens, and the answer is usually that the leak is not through the membrane at all: a rooftop mechanical unit leaking from its own plumbing, a HVAC condensate discharge, a wall flashing above the roof line, or condensation forming on a cold surface inside the building. The report names what was tested and what was not, so the elimination sequence continues to the right place rather than repeating the same test.

What is commercial roof leak detection

A diagnostic to find where water enters a commercial roof membrane. On a low-slope commercial roof water enters at a breach in the membrane and then tracks laterally, so it appears inside the building a long way from where it got in. The instrumented methods find the breach directly, not by tracing the drip.

Why not just follow the drip inside

Because on a low-slope commercial roof the drip inside is rarely directly below the breach. Water enters at the breach, goes through the insulation or along the top of the deck, and appears wherever it finds an opening. Following the drip usually traces to the wrong place. Residential steep-slope roofs behave differently; the residential leak detection page is written around those.

What is electronic leak detection

A method that energises a roof membrane with a small voltage and locates the breach where current passes through it. ASTM D7877 is the standard guide covering both low- and high-voltage electronic methods; ASTM D8231 is the more specific standard practice for the low-voltage scanning form. Both are accepted industry standards.

What is the controlled flood test

A method that dams a defined area of roof, floods it to a controlled depth, and watches the interior for water passing through the membrane. It is definitive on the area tested, and it is limited by the roof structure carrying the flood load and by tenant sensitivity to the interior water it may produce.

Which method is right for my roof

A survey question. ELD is faster on a large open low-slope roof with an electrically insulating membrane. Controlled flood is definitive on a smaller defined area or on membranes ELD cannot read. On a roof where the answer matters enough, both methods run together, ELD first to localise and flood second to confirm.

When does ELD not work

When the membrane is conductive across its whole surface (foil-faced systems), when there is no wet insulation or deck for the current to return through, or when the membrane manufacturer has a specific statement against the method on its product. In any of those the controlled flood is the fallback.

Is a leak detection visit a moisture survey

No. Leak detection finds where water enters the membrane. Moisture survey measures how much of the insulation below the membrane is now wet. They are two different questions with two different methods, and the moisture survey lives on the commercial roof inspections page. On a real leak call the two usually run together, because a wet insulation zone changes what the repair scope needs to be.
