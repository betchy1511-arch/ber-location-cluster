Harahan commercial elastomeric coatings at a glance

An elastomeric coating is a flexible film that stretches with the assembly underneath rather than cracking. Three chemistries dominate the commercial market, and each is a good answer to a different question. Choosing between them is a survey question rather than a brand question, and the leading failure mode on the roof decides it before the datasheet does.

Failure mode one

Ponding water

A low slope commercial roof in a hot humid climate almost always holds water somewhere after rain. Silicone cures by moisture and tolerates standing water once cured. Acrylic coatings are water dispersed and rehydrate under ponding, so an acrylic film under a bird bath softens and eventually blisters. Where ponding is present, silicone is normally the answer.

Failure mode two

Ultraviolet load

Louisiana is a hot humid Zone 2A climate under the state energy code, and ultraviolet load is one of the reasons a coating is specified in the first place. SRI is Solar Reflectance Index calculated under ASTM E1980 from solar reflectance and thermal emittance; CRRC testing is per ANSI/CRRC S100. Aged reflectance and weathering resistance vary by chemistry AND by product, so the comparison that matters is on the specific manufacturer ratings rather than a chemistry-wide claim.

Failure mode three

Foot traffic

Every commercial roof has some foot traffic, and roofs with rooftop mechanical equipment have designated access lanes. Traffic performance varies by chemistry AND by product formulation, and polyurethane systems are commonly specified as walkway or wear surfaces on foam roofs and high-traffic areas. Silicone can be slippery when wet. Traffic lanes are usually specified as the manufacturer's approved walkway system for the field coating, so the chemistry interface is one the manufacturer has approved.

The trap

Re-coat compatibility

A silicone coating usually narrows future re-coat options: silicone's low surface energy is difficult to prime over, so silicone-over-silicone is the reliable path and other chemistries over silicone require specific manufacturer approval, adhesion testing and the correct primer. Acrylic and polyurethane have wider re-coat compatibility. Choosing silicone today usually decides the chemistry family of future re-coats.

The substrate

Filters the shortlist

Not every chemistry adheres to every substrate. Silicone bonds to most existing coatings including its own with the correct wash; acrylic needs a sound, clean substrate and a primer over metal; polyurethane is standard on spray polyurethane foam and needs the correct primer over most single-ply membranes. The substrate on the roof filters the shortlist before the failure modes rank what remains.

The warranty

Written per chemistry

Each chemistry manufacturer writes its own warranty against a specified dry film and a stated preparation, and the terms differ between chemistries and between manufacturers. Ask what the term is written against rather than reading the number of years on its own. The arithmetic behind the dry film sits on a sibling page and is not restated here.

The default assumption on a commercial elastomeric coating in this market is that a silicone coating is what a low slope roof needs because it tolerates the ponding water most of these roofs hold. That assumption is right often enough that it feels like a rule, and it is wrong often enough that specifying against it is the point of this page. Silicone is a good answer to the ponding question. It is a middling answer to the traffic question and a compromise answer to the ultraviolet question, and the roof rarely gets to choose only one of the three.

So the survey ranks the failure modes on the specific roof before the shortlist of chemistries is drawn. Where ponding is the leading failure mode, silicone in the field with the manufacturer's approved walkway system along the access lane is one common answer. Where the roof drains and the ultraviolet load is the primary concern, acrylic can be right, especially on a metal substrate. Where the roof is spray polyurethane foam and there is real traffic on it, a polyurethane system is commonly specified over the foam. What settles it is the roof, and that is what the survey is for.

Re-coat compatibility, and why the first coat locks the future

When an elastomeric coating reaches the end of its film life, the next coat has to bond to what is left of the first one. Recoat compatibility is a chemistry question decided by the coating going on today rather than the one needed in fifteen years, and adhesion always confirms with the manufacturer of the incoming system.

Silicone

Recoat usually stays silicone

Silicone has low surface energy, which is what makes it hold up under ponding water and also difficult to prime over. Silicone-over-silicone with the correct preparation is the standard recoat path. Non-silicone chemistries over silicone require specific manufacturer approval and adhesion testing, and are not the default. Choosing silicone today usually narrows future recoat options to silicone.

Acrylic

Wider recoat compatibility

A sound acrylic film usually accepts silicone, polyurethane and another acrylic on recoat, subject to product-specific adhesion checks, primer requirements and the correct cleaning and preparation. The tradeoff is on the pond and traffic columns, where acrylic is the weakest.

Polyurethane

Wider recoat compatibility

Polyurethane usually accepts most recoats with the correct primer. Aromatic polyurethane is normally top-coated with an aliphatic polyurethane or an acrylic within a defined window because the base itself is not UV stable. The traffic performance is what usually specifies it in the first place.

Where a chemistry choice comes back to cost you

A silicone roof coated ten to fifteen years ago that has reached the end of its film life presents two options, and both are chemistry-decided. Silicone over silicone with the correct wash is the standard answer and the cheaper one. A full removal to bare substrate is possible and expensive, and it is the only route back to acrylic or polyurethane over that roof.

So the silicone choice today is a commitment to silicone as long as the roof stands. That is normally the right commitment on a low slope commercial roof that ponds, and it is worth being direct about at the specification stage rather than discovering it as a surprise fifteen years on.

How the survey ranks the failure modes

Every roof gets three questions, not a preference. Where does water stand after rain, how much traffic does the roof see, and what is the ultraviolet load on the unshaded field. The answers rank the three failure modes on that specific roof, and the ranking selects the chemistry before the manufacturer or the price is named.

Where the survey identifies ponding as the leading failure mode, silicone often finishes at the top of the shortlist. On a metal manufacturing building that drains fully after rain the ranking often runs UV first and traffic second, and acrylic on the field with a manufacturer-approved walkway system on the access lanes can be a better answer than silicone. Neither result is a rule; both come out of the survey on that specific roof.

Ponding and UV on a Louisiana commercial roof

Two climate features sharpen the chemistry question on any Harahan-address low slope commercial roof: rain patterns that can leave water standing on aging low slope roofs, and a Louisiana sun that keeps the ultraviolet load on the film high year round.

Ponding where the survey finds it

Older low slope commercial roofs commonly develop drainage settlement, and where that has happened, water stands after rain even on roofs originally specified to drain fully. That does not mean the whole roof ponds; it usually means specific zones do, and those zones are where an incompatible chemistry fails first.

The scope written from a survey identifies where the water stands, and the chemistry is specified against those zones rather than against the average field. On a building with defined ponding zones, silicone across the ponded areas plus another chemistry in the field is one answer; silicone across the whole field is another. What decides it is the pattern the survey finds and the ponding criterion the coating manufacturer publishes.

UV load and the reflectivity trade

Louisiana is a hot humid Zone 2A climate under the state energy code, and the case for a reflective roof runs on cooling load rather than aesthetics. A cool roof rated by the CRRC (SRI calculated under ASTM E1980; testing per ANSI/CRRC S100) reduces heat gain into the assembly and cuts the summer runtime on rooftop HVAC. Acrylic tends to keep its reflectance longer on a field roof; silicone can start equally reflective and drop faster as dirt collects on the low-surface-energy film, so cleaning is a maintenance discipline rather than an option on a reflectivity-driven silicone spec.

Traffic paths are usually a separate spec

Rooftop equipment on a commercial building sees regular access. Where a designated traffic lane runs between an access door and a piece of equipment, the answer is often not to change the whole roof chemistry but to specify the manufacturer's approved walkway system along that lane. It carries the abrasion where it happens rather than paying for a harder chemistry across square footage that never sees a boot.

Which elastomeric chemistry is right for my roof

It follows from the leading failure mode on the roof. Where water stands after rain, silicone typically tolerates the ponding better than water-dispersed acrylic. Where the roof drains fully and ultraviolet load is the primary concern, acrylic often wins on reflectance retention. Where foot traffic is heavy, especially over spray polyurethane foam, a polyurethane system is commonly specified. Every roof gets its own ranking; there is no universal answer.

Why not silicone on every roof

Because the ponding tolerance that makes silicone the right answer over standing water is also what makes almost nothing else stick to it on a re-coat. Silicone usually narrows future recoat options to silicone. On a roof that drains fully, choosing silicone anyway means paying that compatibility cost for a benefit the roof never uses. On a roof that ponds, it is normally the right trade.

Can I mix chemistries on the same roof

Yes, and it is a common answer where the failure modes vary across the roof. Silicone in the ponding zones with acrylic in the field roof is one pattern; acrylic across the field with the manufacturer's approved walkway system along the traffic lane is another. Any interface between chemistries requires the incoming manufacturer's approval and adhesion testing, and the recoat compatibility is noted on the file.

What is the leading failure mode on a low slope Louisiana roof

Ponding water is a common leading failure mode. A hot humid climate delivers heavy rain and slow drying, and drainage settlement over the life of a commercial roof can create zones where water stands after rain even on a roof originally specified to drain. Whether ponding is the leading mode on a specific roof is a survey question, because the pattern varies from building to building.

What does ultraviolet actually do to a coating

Different things to different chemistries. Acrylic can harden and eventually craze once its reflectance has dropped; silicone tends to collect dirt on its low-surface-energy film so its initial reflectance falls; aromatic polyurethane degrades under UV without a top coat, which is why aliphatic polyurethane or another UV-stable finish is normally used. None of the three is UV-proof; the question is which mechanism the specific roof can live with, and manufacturer product ratings are how it is compared.

Is a coating with high SRI enough on its own

SRI is Solar Reflectance Index calculated under ASTM E1980 from solar reflectance and thermal emittance; CRRC testing is per ANSI/CRRC S100. It is one input, not the whole answer. On a hot humid roof, reflectivity reduces heat gain into the assembly and saves cooling energy; on the same roof it does nothing about ponding water or foot traffic. SRI is a benefit on the UV column; it does not substitute for ponding tolerance or abrasion resistance.

Can I re-coat a silicone roof with acrylic

Not as a default. Silicone's low surface energy is difficult to prime over, so acrylic over silicone is possible only with specific manufacturer approval, adhesion testing, and the correct primer. It is not the standard answer. The reliable recoat path on a silicone roof is silicone with the correct preparation, and that is worth planning for at the initial specification rather than treating as a surprise.

When should a commercial coating be re-coated

Sooner than the failure mode of the specific chemistry demands. Waiting until the film has failed on a re-coat schedule is waiting too long, because the underlying covering is exposed as soon as the film fails and the re-coat is not repairing that damage. The film thickness is measured periodically and the re-coat is scheduled against the specification the manufacturer warranty rides on, not against a calendar interval.
